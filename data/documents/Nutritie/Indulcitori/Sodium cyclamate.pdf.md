# Sodium cyclamate.pdf

## Pagini

### Pagina 1

Sodium cyclamate, called cyclamate in most safety documents, is a high-intensity
artificial sweetener used in some drinks, tabletop sweeteners, desserts, and other low-
sugar products. In the EU it is authorized as food additive E 952, while in the United
States it remains banned for use in foods. WHO/JECFA lists sodium cyclamate as a
sweetener with an ADI, the EU still allows cyclamates, and FDA’s standing decision
from 1980 kept cyclamate off the U.S. food market.
The first thing to understand is that cyclamate’s safety story is not “totally proven
harmless forever” and it is also not “obvious poison.” The more accurate picture is this:
regulators have historically considered cyclamate acceptable within intake limits, mainly
because the strongest direct human evidence for serious harm has been weak, but
some older toxicology concerns never fully disappeared, especially around its
metabolite cyclohexylamine and around the fact that parts of the genotoxicity data set
are old or methodologically weak. That is why you see different regulatory outcomes
and why EFSA’s modern re-evaluation is still ongoing.
What happens in the body is important here. Cyclamate is not fully absorbed from the
gut. A large share is excreted unchanged, mostly in urine and feces, but some of the
unabsorbed cyclamate can be converted by gut bacteria into cyclohexylamine. Most
people convert only a small fraction, but the amount varies a lot between individuals,
and some people can convert much more than others. That variability is one of the main
reasons cyclamate has always been assessed more cautiously than a simple “non-
metabolized sweetener.”
The current intake limits reflect that caution. WHO/JECFA lists an ADI of 0–11 mg/kg
body weight per day, expressed as cyclamic acid. The old EU Scientific Committee on
Food first used that same temporary ADI, then in 2000 tightened it to 0–7 mg/kg body
weight per day after revising assumptions about how much cyclamate some users may
convert to cyclohexylamine. The committee based that limit on a NOAEL of 100 mg/kg
bw/day for testicular toxicity of cyclohexylamine in rats, then applied conversion
assumptions and safety factors.
That detail matters because the main long-standing toxicology concern is not
cyclamate itself in some dramatic Hollywood sense, but the fact that its metabolite
cyclohexylamine caused testicular toxicity in rats at sufficient doses. The old SCF
opinion says this directly, and Health Canada’s later assessment also points to rat
testicular findings and supports the same NOAEL of 100 mg/kg bw/day from those
studies. IARC’s monograph likewise notes that cyclamates and cyclohexylamine can
produce testicular toxicity in rats.
That does not mean normal human intake causes infertility or testicular damage. It
means that, in animal studies, the metabolite had a real toxic effect at high enough
doses, so regulators used that endpoint to set conservative limits. Human evidence has

not shown a clear fertility signal. In the SCF review, the human case-control study on
cyclamate intake and male fertility found no statistically significant difference in
estimated cyclamate intake or urinary cyclamate excretion between infertility cases and
controls. A small worker study was too limited and confounded to be very informative.
Now the cancer question, because that is where most of the panic comes from. The
short version is that the scary reputation came mostly from older animal work,
especially bladder-cancer concerns from the late 1960s and from combination studies
involving cyclamate and saccharin. But the better human evidence has not shown a
convincing cancer signal. IARC’s review of the human data states that the few
epidemiologic studies that analyzed cyclamates specifically found no increase in
urinary bladder cancer risk, and its summary explicitly says no increase in bladder-
cancer risk was seen in those human studies.
The animal cancer data were mixed and messy rather than cleanly damning. IARC’s
summary says oral administration studies in mice and rats generally did not show a
treatment-related increase in tumor incidence in the evaluable studies, while several
other animal experiments were judged inadequate or incompletely reported. It also
notes that bladder tumors seen in implantation studies were not useful for determining
carcinogenicity. In other words, the animal record was complicated enough to create
concern, but not strong enough to amount to a clean, reproducible “cyclamate clearly
causes cancer” conclusion.
There was also a very long nonhuman primate study published in 2000. The SCF
explicitly said that study was inconclusive and that it could not be used for risk
assessment. The paper itself reported a few malignant tumors in very old cyclamate-
treated monkeys, but that by itself did not establish a clear causal carcinogenic effect,
and regulators did not treat it as decisive evidence.
So the claim that cyclamate “gives you cancer” is not supported the way people on the
internet often say it is. A stronger and more accurate statement is this: older toxicology
findings raised cancer concerns, especially in rodents and in historical regulatory
reviews, but the available human evidence has not shown a clear carcinogenic
effect from normal dietary use. That is why international bodies kept ADIs rather than
concluding that any exposure is unacceptable, while the U.S. kept its ban under its own
legal and evidentiary framework.
On genotoxicity, the picture is also more nuanced than internet certainty. EFSA has said
that the studies on cyclamates and cyclohexylamine published after IARC’s 1999
evaluation are of limited relevance and/or reliability, and that some positive findings
in older in vitro and in vivo studies had methodological shortcomings. EFSA therefore
asked for additional in vivo Comet assay data for cyclamates and cyclohexylamine to
move the assessment forward. That means the right conclusion is not “genotoxicity

proven” and not “genotoxicity impossible,” but rather “the modern evidence package is
not as complete and robust as EFSA would like.”
On reproductive and developmental toxicity more broadly, the evidence is less alarming
than people claim. IARC’s review says cyclamates were not observed to produce
developmental toxicity in mice, rats, rabbits, hamsters, dogs, or rhesus monkeys, and
were not teratogenic in mice, rats, or rhesus monkeys, though some studies suggested
reduced embryo growth or viability signals. That is not the same thing as saying
pregnancy exposure is ideal or that more is always fine; it means the evidence does not
support a simple “birth-defect chemical” narrative.
There is also the issue of placental transfer. Older studies cited in later official reviews
found that cyclamate can cross the placenta and distribute to fetal tissues. That sounds
scary when quoted alone, but placental transfer by itself is not proof of harm. It just
means that fetal exposure is biologically possible, which is one reason regulators
traditionally preferred conservative intake limits rather than a casual “unlimited use”
stance.
For real-world exposure, official assessments generally do not show catastrophic intake
patterns in the general population. The 2000 SCF opinion reviewed intake surveys
showing average daily intake among consumers in the low single-digit mg/kg/day range,
with only a very small fraction of surveyed people estimated above the then-temporary
ADI. Health Canada’s screening assessment later concluded that current margins of
exposure for sodium cyclamate were adequate and that sodium cyclamate and
cyclohexylamine did not pose a danger to human life or health under the conditions
assessed in Canada.
That said, “safe within limits” is not the same as “completely impossible to have adverse
effects.” The adverse effects that are plausible in principle are the ones regulators
already built the ADI around: high exposure in a person who converts a lot of cyclamate
to cyclohexylamine, over time, could theoretically push internal exposure toward levels
of concern derived from animal studies. But this is exactly what the ADI is designed to
stay well below. What is not supported is the dramatic online claim that small normal
amounts are basically a cancer grenade.
A useful way to sort the claims is this. “Cyclamate definitely causes cancer in humans”
is not supported by the better human evidence. “Cyclamate has zero unresolved
issues and absolutely nothing left to study” is also not true, because EFSA’s re-
evaluation is still open and genotoxicity data gaps remain. “Cyclamate’s main historical
concern is conversion to cyclohexylamine, with testicular toxicity in rats driving the
intake limit” is well supported. “Normal dietary use under established ADIs has been
regarded by several regulators as acceptable” is also well supported.

### Pagina 4

So, if you want the blunt conclusion: sodium cyclamate is not proven to be a human
carcinogen from normal food use, and the “stage 5 cancer” talk is garbage. But it is
also not one of those additives where every possible concern has been fully buried
forever. The scientifically honest position is that cyclamate has a long, messy toxicology
history; its key concern is the metabolite cyclohexylamine; the ADI was set
conservatively around that issue; human cancer evidence has been reassuring rather
than damning; and some data gaps, especially around genotoxicity by current
standards, are still being cleaned up.
